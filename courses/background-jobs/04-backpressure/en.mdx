## The problem

A marketing campaign sends 200,000 users to your site in an hour. Each sign-up adds three jobs. Your workers finish **40 jobs a second**, but jobs arrive at **160 a second**.

The API stays fast, so nothing looks wrong. But the queue grows by 120 jobs every second. After the hour, about 430,000 jobs are waiting: welcome emails arrive **three hours late**, Redis is running low on memory, and password-reset emails are stuck behind all of them.

## The idea

A queue hides overload: it absorbs a burst, which is good, but a queue that grows **all the time** is a problem building up silently.

Watch two numbers:

- **Queue depth**: how many jobs are waiting. Useful, but a big number might be fine.
- **Age of the oldest job**: how long the next job has been waiting. This is what users feel. "Emails are 3 hours late" is the real alert.

When producers outrun workers, you have three options. Pick on purpose:

| Option | How | Good when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Add capacity** | More workers, higher concurrency | The limit is your workers, not a downstream service |
| **Slow producers** (backpressure) | Rate-limit or reject new work, return `429` or "try later" | The work can wait at its source |
| **Drop or defer work** | Skip low-value jobs, or run them at night | Some jobs matter much less than others |

And **separate urgent work from bulk work**: give password resets their own queue, so they never wait behind a campaign.

## Example

Separate queues, so urgent jobs have their own workers:

```ts
const critical = new Queue('critical', { connection: redis }); // password resets, receipts
const bulk = new Queue('bulk', { connection: redis }); // newsletters, CRM sync

new Worker('critical', runJob, { connection: redis, concurrency: 20 });
new Worker('bulk', runJob, { connection: redis, concurrency: 5 });
```

Measure the age of the oldest waiting job and alert on it:

```ts
setInterval(async () => {
  const [oldest] = await bulk.getWaiting(0, 0);
  const ageSeconds = oldest ? (Date.now() - oldest.timestamp) / 1000 : 0;
  metrics.gauge('queue.oldest_job_age_seconds', ageSeconds, { queue: 'bulk' });
}, 15_000);
```

Alert when the critical queue's oldest job is older than 1 minute, and the bulk queue's older than 1 hour. During the campaign, password resets keep arriving in seconds while the newsletter catches up overnight.

## Try it

1. For one queue you run, find out how fast jobs arrive and how fast workers finish them at peak.
2. Add a metric for the age of the oldest waiting job.
3. Decide the maximum acceptable delay for that queue, and alert on it.
4. If urgent and bulk jobs share a queue, split them.

## Check yourself

**1. Jobs arrive at 100 per second and workers finish 80 per second. How much does the queue grow in an hour?**

<Answer>
20 jobs per second × 3,600 seconds = 72,000 jobs an hour, and it keeps growing until arrivals drop below 80 per second or you add capacity.
</Answer>

**2. Why is the age of the oldest job a better alert than queue depth?**

<Answer>
It measures what users experience: how late the work is. 10,000 tiny jobs might clear in seconds, while 50 slow ones might already be hours late.
</Answer>

**3. Your workers spend most of their time waiting on an email provider that allows 50 sends a second. Will more workers help?**

<Answer>
No. The bottleneck is the provider's limit, so more workers only hit the limit harder. Slow the producers, spread the work out, or send low-priority emails later.
</Answer>

## Key takeaways

- A queue that **grows all the time** is overload you can't see yet.
- Alert on the **age of the oldest job**, not only on depth.
- Choose on purpose: **add capacity, slow producers, or drop and defer work**.
- Keep **urgent work in its own queue**.
